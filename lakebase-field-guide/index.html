01 / Start here

Lakebase in 60 seconds

What is Lakebase, and why does a customer care?

Lakebase is fully managed Postgres inside Databricks. Developers get a standard Postgres database (versions 16, 17 or 18). The data team gets that operational data next to the lakehouse, with supported paths in and out.

Why it wins

  • Apps and agents read curated lakehouse data through managed synced tables, not custom reverse-ETL jobs.
  • Copy-on-write branches give every developer or CI run an isolated, production-shaped database in seconds.
  • No database servers to run: autoscaling, optional scale-to-zero, zone-redundant HA, point-in-time restore and no I/O meter.

Lead with

  • Agent state and memory
  • Serving lakehouse data to an app
  • Dev/test and CI environments
  • New apps on Databricks, and Postgres apps ready to move
  • Long term: the database estate, wave by wave

Slow down when

  • Production cross-region DR is mandatory today
  • One writer must exceed 112 CU, or writes must shard
  • Must-have extensions aren't supported, or superuser access or Private Link-only replication is required
  • The current database is healthy and nobody owns a reason to change

“Lakebase is the Postgres your developers already know, with branching, autoscaling and lakehouse data a sync away, and no database servers to run.”

Find the app or platform owner, not just the data team: they make the database decision. Qualify one workload with Discovery, then bring in your SA.

Working with your SA

When does the AE bring in the SA, and with what?

As soon as there's a named app and an owner. Bring six facts so the first technical call designs the proof instead of rediscovering the workload.

The AE brings

  1. The app, its owner and the decision date.
  2. The current database: engine, version, size, cloud, region, and what hurts.
  3. Load: peak transactions or queries per second, connections, and the p95/p99 latency target.
  4. Availability: HA, RPO/RTO, any cross-region requirement.
  5. Security: Private Link, customer-managed keys, compliance standard.
  6. Budget: today's database and integration spend.

The SA returns

  • The data path and architecture: synced tables, CDF or logical replication.
  • Sizing and a shared calculator scenario.
  • A proof plan with pass/fail criteria.
  • A migration and rollback plan.
  • Gates and risks, in writing.

Escalate together on hard regional DR, more than 112 CU on one writer, unsupported extensions, replication that must use Private Link, or a compliance standard the target region doesn't support. For a database-estate program, bring a migration partner in early.

Agree a dated proof plan with the customer: one workload, success criteria, owners and a decision date.

02 / Fit

Discovery

Where should we lead, and when should we leave the database alone?

Lead where an app needs Postgres plus lakehouse data, agent state or fast test environments. A healthy database with no pain is a weak takeout target: win the next app instead.

“Which part of your app lifecycle is expensive today: serving lakehouse data, remembering agent state, creating test environments, or running a fleet of small databases?”

  1. Who owns the app and the production decision? Listen for an app or platform team, a DBA and a security owner, not only the data team.
  2. How does lakehouse data reach the app today? Listen for nightly exports, custom reverse-ETL jobs, or a cache in front of the warehouse.
  3. What will grow? More apps and teams (a platform default), or one service with more traffic.
  4. What must production prove? p95/p99 latency, concurrency, freshness, RPO/RTO, residency and an acceptable monthly bill.
  5. What is fixed? Cloud and region, Private Link, compliance standard, required extensions and DR.

Strong signals

  • Apps or agents being built on Databricks
  • Jobs copying Delta tables into RDS or Aurora
  • Many small Postgres databases, or Postgres that idles most of the day
  • Slow or shared test environments

Weak signals

  • One steady database that works and has no owner asking for change
  • MySQL, SQL Server or Oracle apps with no engine change planned
  • Production cross-region DR required now

Displacement must beat migration risk. Hard regional DR, extension or hardware requirements can end the opportunity before a benchmark.

Leave with one workload, its owner, its current cost or pain, and a dated proof plan. Check the decision gates first.

Decision gates

Which requirements stop or reshape the opportunity?

Check six gates before investing in a proof. Each one has a supported answer, a reshaped scope, or a reason to walk away.

  1. Production cross-region DR, now. DR is in Private Preview and not intended for production. Cross-region logical replication is supported but customer-managed. Reshape to workloads that don't need regional failover, or get explicit sign-off on a design.
  2. Writes beyond one primary. There is one writer per branch, up to 112 CU fixed. Horizontal write scale-out needs a distributed design; Aurora Limitless and Aurora DSQL are separate products, not upgrades of every Aurora app.
  3. Compatibility. Extensions not on the supported list (pg_cron, for example), superuser or OS access, custom tablespaces. Resolve these before promising a migration.
  4. Network and compliance. Logical replication runs over public networking only, without Private Link. Some standards are region-specific: ISMAP in Tokyo, IRAP in Sydney. Dedicated physical hosts need an explicit architecture attestation.
  5. Quotas. Storage quota per branch is adjustable on request, not a fixed ceiling. Projects default to 20 concurrently active computes. Confirm both before large loads or CI fan-out.
  6. No reason to change. No measurable pain on a healthy system: start with a new adjacent workload.

Lead plays

Which workloads make a credible first win?

Start with a specific app problem and an owner who can take it to production. Four plays cover most first wins. SaaS backends and spiky portfolios are deployment shapes for these plays, not separate reasons to move.

01 / Agent state & memory

Problem: agents lose progress, forget preferences or double-claim tasks.

Pitch: durable state, checkpoints and retrieval in one Postgres, next to the data the agent reasons over.

Talk to: the team building the agent or app.

Design: transactional state and checkpoints in Postgres, with optional vector and keyword indexes for long-term memory. Keep thread state separate from cross-session memory.

Boundary: retries, idempotency, tenant authorization and retrieval relevance remain app responsibilities.

Prove: restart recovery, concurrent task ownership, retrieval quality and p99 latency.

02 / Lakehouse serving

Problem: an app needs low-latency SQL on curated lakehouse data alongside its own writes.

Pitch: managed synced tables keep a Postgres serving copy current, with no reverse-ETL jobs to run.

Talk to: the app team and the data team that owns the tables.

Design: synced tables create a read-only serving copy; application writes go to separate tables.

Boundary: validate source keys, Delta CDF for triggered and continuous modes, additive schema evolution, freshness and pipeline DBUs.

Prove: initial load, incremental lag, serving p99 and the full integration cost.

03 / Dev/test & CI branches

Problem: test databases take too long to prepare, or developers share mutable state.

Pitch: an isolated, production-shaped branch per developer or CI run in seconds, expiring automatically.

Talk to: platform engineering, DevOps and the DBA.

Design: copy-on-write branches with expiration, and scale-to-zero for idle endpoints.

Boundary: branches don't merge. Promote tested schema changes with migrations and protect production data with masking and access rules.

Prove: environment readiness, reset, masked access, cleanup and active-compute quotas.

04 / Postgres app migration

Problem: Postgres apps on RDS, Aurora or self-managed servers, with lakehouse data moved in and out by custom jobs.

Pitch: replicate into Lakebase with native logical replication, run in parallel, and cut over when the numbers pass.

Talk to: the app owner and the DBA.

Design: load the schema, subscribe from Lakebase, then replace custom pipelines with synced tables or CDF.

Boundary: the source needs a public endpoint; replication doesn't use Private Link, copies data but not schema, and sequences must be reset at cutover.

Prove: replication lag, a rehearsed cutover, the rollback path and p99 after cutover.

03 / Big bet

Big bet · Database estate

Why move the database estate to Lakebase, and why now?

Over two to three years, move the long tail of application databases onto Lakebase, wave by wave: Postgres and MySQL first, then SQL Server and Oracle apps. Don't open with the Tier-1 core system. Win the many smaller databases around it; every app that moves lands next to the lakehouse and AI.

Cost and risk

  • Stop paying to keep old versions alive: license and support renewals, extended-support surcharges and like-for-like upgrade projects.
  • No database servers, patching or HA plumbing to run.

Speed

  • A production-shaped branch for every developer, test run and migration rehearsal.
  • Standard Postgres skills, drivers and tools.

Data and AI

  • Operational data is a sync away from the lakehouse: synced tables in, change data feed out.
  • Apps and agents get state and context on one governed platform, without reverse-ETL glue.

Why now: dated triggers to look for

  • SQL Server: 2016 extended support ended on 14 Jul 2026; 2017 ends on 12 Oct 2027.
  • MySQL: 8.0 reached community end of life on 30 Apr 2026. Amazon RDS for MySQL 8.0 left standard support on 31 Jul 2026, so databases still on it pay Extended Support charges.
  • PostgreSQL: 13 is past end of life, with RDS Extended Support charges since 1 Mar 2026. 14 reaches end of life on 12 Nov 2026 and leaves RDS standard support on 28 Feb 2027.
  • Oracle: support renewals, license audits and data-center exits. Time each wave to a renewal date.

“Let's stop paying to keep old databases alive. Move them app by app onto Postgres you don't have to run, right next to your data and AI.”

“We'll lift and shift your Oracle core,” or “no code changes.” Engine migrations rewrite schema, procedures and some application code.

Lakebase fits application databases, not every high-end Tier-1 system. Keep high-volume cores with heavy PL/SQL or T-SQL, writes beyond one primary, or mandatory cross-region failover out of the first waves, and possibly out of scope.

Ask for two documents: the database inventory and the license and support renewal calendar. Then book an estate triage.

Technical detail: move the read side first

For an app that can't move in one cutover, start with its reads. Lakeflow Connect lands the legacy database in Delta: CDC connectors for SQL Server, MySQL and PostgreSQL (the last two in Public Preview), and query-based connectors for Oracle and other sources. Synced tables then serve curated tables or views over that data from Lakebase to new or rewritten modules, and reporting moves to the lakehouse on day one. Write paths move module by module; retire the legacy database when the last one has moved.

Technical detail: target checks
  • Capacity: one writer per branch, up to 112 CU fixed; the storage quota is adjustable on request. Bulk-load at a larger size, then scale back.
  • Authentication: migration and CDC tools need a native Postgres password role, because OAuth tokens expire hourly. Enable password connections on the project.
  • Network: tools that write into Lakebase connect inbound like any client, so inbound Private Link works. Only logical replication with Lakebase as the subscriber needs the source on a public endpoint.
  • Extensions: orafce, oracle_fdw, tds_fdw, mysql_fdw, postgres_fdw, dblink, pg_cron and pg_partman are not on the supported list. There is no in-database pull from the source, and scheduled jobs move to Lakeflow Jobs or the app.
  • Procedural code: PL/pgSQL is available. Keep transactional logic in Postgres or the app, and move batch and reporting logic to the lakehouse.
  • Rehearsal: run each conversion and cutover rehearsal on a branch, then reset and repeat until reconciliation (row counts, checksums) and p99 pass.
Tools and accelerators
  • Conversion: open-source ora2pg (Oracle, MySQL) and pgloader (MySQL, SQL Server), the AWS Schema Conversion Tool, and partner AI-assisted converters for PL/SQL and T-SQL.
  • Data and CDC: tools that support a Postgres target, such as AWS DMS, Debezium with a JDBC sink, Oracle GoldenGate or commercial CDC. Validate Lakebase as the target: Postgres 16–18, a password role, the network path and throughput.
  • Lakebase Express: an open-source Databricks Solutions app that assesses, sizes, converts and loads SQL Server and Azure SQL databases into Lakebase. It is not an official Databricks product and has no support or SLA, so run it on non-production data first.
  • Partners: Databricks lists partner accelerators that automate assessment, schema conversion and code migration from Oracle and SQL Server, using Lakebase branching to rehearse cutovers.

04 / Technical FAQ

Data paths & governance

Is it one copy of the data, with no pipelines?

It depends who owns the writes. Lakebase has four supported data paths. Choose one per table, then agree its freshness target and permissions before promising an architecture.

“We can remove most of the custom integration work. First we choose the supported path for each table, its freshness target, and who connects to it.”

“Zero ETL, zero copies everywhere,” or “Unity Catalog governs the Postgres tables.”

Technical detail: copies, freshness and authorization
  • Synced tables (GA): a managed, read-only Postgres copy maintained by Lakeflow pipelines that bill serverless pipeline DBUs. Snapshot, triggered or continuous modes; continuous keeps the copy within seconds of the source. Triggered and continuous need Delta CDF on the source, and schema evolution is additive. Write app state to separate tables. LTAP Direct Writes (Beta, opt-in at creation) speeds initial loads and full refreshes by writing to storage directly.
  • Lakebase CDF (Public Preview): captures Postgres row changes from the WAL into Unity Catalog Delta history tables, batched about every 15 seconds. A change log still needs downstream logic to derive current state, and the flush cadence is not an end-to-end freshness SLA. A workspace admin enables the preview.
  • Lakehouse//RT over Lakebase (Private Preview): transactionally consistent analytical queries on live Postgres data, isolated from the primary's OLTP work. It needs private-preview access and a supported cloud/region; it is not unrestricted Spark access to Postgres storage files.
  • Logical replication: Postgres-to-Postgres, in and out. It copies data, not schema, runs on the primary compute, and uses public networking only. See Migration.

Permissions have two surfaces: Unity Catalog governs analytical access to registered Lakebase data; direct Postgres clients use Postgres roles and GRANT/REVOKE. A Unity Catalog row filter or mask does not become a Postgres policy. Synced-table ownership also restricts owner-only operations, such as configuring RLS directly on the synced table.

Cache warmth

Does autoscaling make the cache cold?

No. Scaling within the configured range happens in place, without restarting Postgres or dropping connections. Scaling down can shrink the cache and a suspend clears it, so size the minimum for the hot working set.

“Set the floor to your working set and keep scale-to-zero off for latency-critical apps. Then the cache stays warm.”

“The cache never goes cold.” Suspension, restarts and scheduled updates reset compute-local state.

Technical detail: the four-layer read path
1Shared buffers: Postgres pages in compute DRAM.
2Local file cache (LFC): a resizable, disk-backed compute cache; the Linux page cache can keep its hot pages in RAM.
3Pageserver: materializes page versions from WAL and serves compute-cache misses.
4Object storage: durable storage beneath the pageserver, independent of the compute lifetime.

How is LFC different from ordinary NVMe caching?

Remote page reads bypass the usual local-file read path. LFC supplies a local cache below shared buffers that can resize while Postgres runs. A hot LFC hit can come from the OS page cache; a miss may need NVMe or a pageserver request. NVMe is a medium, not a guarantee that every query stays local.

Large fixed computes: the 9 Sep 2026 engineering report says fixed endpoints of 80 CU or more disable LFC and give 75% of DRAM to shared buffers, using huge pages, so an 80 CU endpoint should report 120GB from SHOW shared_buffers;. The report describes dynamic shared buffers for autoscaling as future work, so verify the endpoint rather than extending the claim to every size.

Suspension or restart loses compute-local state; storage persists. A surviving storage cache can help, but its contents and first-query latency are not guaranteed. Scheduled updates and failure recovery are separate from routine autoscaling.

Measure compute-cache hit rate, storage reads and p99 latency while scaling down and after a suspend. Set the minimum CU for the hot working set plus query and session memory, not the whole database size.

Network & latency

Doesn't separated storage add latency at high QPS?

Cache hits stay local; cache misses and commits do network work, as in any separated-storage database, Aurora included. The real question is whether the working set, write rate and client location meet the app's latency target.

“Let's test your expensive cases (cold scans, hot rows, sustained writes) at your durability and latency target.”

Your SA designs a benchmark with the customer's schema, data size and client region.

Technical detail: read and commit paths 
Read:   shared buffers → LFC → pageserver → object storage
Commit: Postgres WAL → safekeeper quorum (Paxos) → client acknowledgment
        pageserver materialization happens asynchronously

Compute asks a pageserver for a page at an LSN, and the pageserver reconstructs that version if needed. A commit waits for a quorum of safekeepers to acknowledge the WAL record, not for the final page image to reach object storage.

Aurora also separates compute and storage, and both use local caching, but their storage protocols differ. A shared architecture does not establish equal latency, throughput or failover behavior.

Benchmark the expensive cases: cold scans beyond the cache, large joins that spill, hot-row contention, cross-region clients and sustained write pressure. A cache-friendly microbenchmark is weak evidence for a scan-heavy application.

Use the same schema, indexes, data size, durability configuration and client location for every candidate. Record committed TPS, p50/p95/p99, errors, cache misses and the monthly cost at the measured capacity.

Write performance

Can we say “5× faster writes than Aurora”?

No. The published 5× compares Lakebase's own write path before and after an engineering change (full-page-image pushdown). It shows an efficient design, not a competitive guarantee.

“There's engineering evidence for a more efficient write path. The proof that matters is your transaction mix at your durability target.”

Technical detail: full-page-image pushdown

Conventional Postgres writes full-page images into WAL after checkpoints to recover from torn pages. Lakebase's separated storage can generate those images in the pageserver instead of sending them from compute, which cuts WAL traffic while bounding the redo needed to rebuild a page.

The engineering benchmark reports up to about 5× higher throughput and 94% less WAL traffic; its 32-vCPU comparison measured about 4.5×. These results describe the tested workloads and the previous implementation. They do not remove locking, index maintenance, query or single-writer limits.

Include WAL-heavy updates, secondary indexes, checkpoint behavior, lock waits and realistic transaction sizes. Treat one writer per branch as a design constraint.

Isolation & security

Does shared storage mean shared access to my data?

No. The storage service is multi-tenant, as with most cloud databases, but each project is an isolated tenant with its own compute VMs, and data is encrypted at rest. Customer-managed keys and Private Link are available. It is not dedicated hardware.

“Your data is isolated per project and encrypted, with your own revocable key if you want it. Lakebase is SOC 2 Type 2, and supports PCI-DSS and HITRUST with the compliance security profile.”

“Single-tenant” or “dedicated hardware.” If procurement requires dedicated hosts, get an explicit architecture attestation.

Technical detail: authorization, keys, networking and compliance
  • Database access: use least-privilege Postgres roles. Project permissions control infrastructure actions; they don't replace database privileges.
  • Customer-managed keys: workspace admins on the Enterprise tier enable CMK at workspace level; new projects are then encrypted with the key. A project marked Not supported can't be converted in place: migrate it to a new project.
  • Key availability: rotation causes no downtime, but revoking or losing access to the key makes every CMK-managed project unavailable. Rehearse the recovery runbook.
  • Private access: a workspace that uses Private Link needs inbound Private Link for Lakebase. Verify the actual route for every client, including external apps. Logical replication is the exception: it runs over public networking, with the account network policy governing outbound connections and the workspace IP access list governing inbound subscribers.
  • Compliance: SOC 2 Type 2. PCI-DSS and HITRUST in every AWS Lakebase region and on Azure, with the compliance security profile. HIPAA, C5 and TISAX profiles are supported. Region-specific: IRAP (ap-southeast-2), ISMAP (ap-northeast-1), UK Cyber Essentials Plus (eu-west-1, eu-west-2).

Run permission tests through both Postgres and lakehouse clients, verify private routes, and rehearse CMK loss and recovery with the security owner.

Connections & retries

Is connection pooling built in, and who connects directly?

Built-in PgBouncer transaction mode · native password roles only; OAuth direct · 10K clients ≠ running transactions

Yes. A built-in PgBouncer runs in transaction mode for native password roles and accepts up to 10,000 client connections. OAuth roles connect directly. 10,000 clients is not 10,000 concurrent transactions.

“Serverless functions and web APIs can use the built-in pooler. Session-heavy tools and replication connect directly.”

Your SA tests the customer's actual driver and ORM, pooled and direct.

Technical detail: pooling, authentication and session behavior 
pool_mode = transaction
max_client_conn = 10000
default_pool_size ≈ 0.9 × max_connections
max_prepared_statements = 1000
query_wait_timeout = 120 seconds

These settings are managed and can't be changed. Copy the -pooler connection string from the Lakebase Connect dialog. Native password connections are disabled by default on new projects; enable them explicitly if the design uses the pooler.

Use direct connections for session-dependent behavior: session-level settings and locks, LISTEN, SQL-level PREPARE, persistent temporary tables, logical replication and admin tools that need session affinity. Protocol-level prepared statements work through the pooler; validate the actual driver and ORM.

OAuth tokens are time-limited, so apps must refresh credentials hourly; tools that can't should use a native password role. Connections idle for 24 hours are closed.

EventClient behavior to design for
Routine autoscaling within rangeNo restart or connection interruption.
Changing min/max configurationMay briefly interrupt connections.
Suspension, restart or scheduled updateSession state resets; reconnect and restore required settings. Planned updates are posted at least 3 days ahead.
HA failoverExisting connections terminate, and connections to a failed primary can hang: set TCP keepalives or connect timeouts, then reconnect to the stable primary endpoint.

Bound connect, query and transaction timeouts, keep transactions short and retry with backoff. A lost commit acknowledgment can leave an unknown outcome: use an idempotency key or reconcile before repeating business writes.

HA, DR & restore

What survives a compute failure, a zone outage or a region outage?

Committed data survives a compute failure, because storage records every commit durably before acknowledging it. HA across availability zones adds automatic in-region failover. Cross-region DR is still in Private Preview and not intended for production, so regional recovery needs its own design.

“We can prove in-region failover today. If production regional recovery is mandatory, we'll design it explicitly before promising anything.”

“DR is included” or “zero downtime.” Failover drops existing connections, and apps reconnect.

Technical detail: readable secondaries, DR recovery and SLA

HA secondaries can serve reads through -ro when read access is enabled. With only one readable secondary, a promotion interrupts that read path until replacement capacity is ready. Dedicated read replicas (up to 6 per branch) have separate endpoints and independent sizing and are eventually consistent; apps that need read-after-write correctness should use the primary.

Regional DR keeps the secondary compute idle until promotion, but replicated storage and transfer have their own cost. Transactions that didn't replicate before a failover are kept as a recovery branch when the original region returns; they aren't reconciled automatically. Rehearse credentials and integration restarts in a drill.

SLA: service credits start below 99.99% monthly uptime for HA endpoints and below 99.9% for single-compute endpoints, on covered connection strings and with exclusions, including use with Beta services. It is not a zero-downtime guarantee or a latency SLA. Azure deployments use the applicable Microsoft SLA.

Branches & promotion

Are branches instant, free and merged automatically?

Copy-on-write (500 / project) · expiring: changed-data billing; permanent: full-data billing

Instant: yes. Free: no. Merged: no. Creation time doesn't depend on database size. Expiring branches bill only changed data, permanent branches bill their full size, and active branch compute bills separately. Changes never flow back automatically.

“Give every developer and CI run an isolated, production-shaped database in seconds, and let it expire on its own.”

A branch copies the data's sensitivity too. Set access, masking and expiry before developers touch production data.

Technical detail: isolation, reset, quotas and billing
  • Branch from current data or any point in the restore window. Each child has its own databases, roles and grants. Reset refreshes a child from its parent in one direction only; apply tested schema changes to production with migration tools.
  • Protected branches can't be deleted or reset and block project deletion, which suits production and staging.
  • Billing: an expiring branch bills the data that changes on it. A permanent branch bills its full data size even though storage is copy-on-write. Active branch computes bill separately.
  • Quotas: 500 branches per project and 20 concurrently active computes by default. Large CI fan-out must budget active endpoints and request quota increases where needed.

Create an expiring branch, run a schema test, reset it and inspect its usage. Compare with Aurora's copy-on-write clone workflow rather than assuming Aurora needs a full dump and restore.

Evidence: Branch isolation & billing · Quotas · Aurora cloning

Migration

Can an RDS, Aurora or self-managed Postgres app move with little downtime?

Yes, for Postgres sources. Since 15 Oct 2026, Lakebase supports native Postgres logical replication: it copies the source and keeps it in sync until cutover. MySQL, SQL Server, Oracle and MongoDB apps need an engine migration: plan those as waves in the database-estate big bet.

“Start with one Postgres app: replicate it into Lakebase, run both in parallel, and cut over when the numbers pass.”

“It moves unchanged.” Check extensions, roles and session features first, and confirm the source can expose a public endpoint for replication.

Your SA runs a compatibility inventory and a replication rehearsal against a copy of the source.

Technical detail: compatibility gates
  • Postgres 16, 17 and 18 are supported (17 is the default). Check extension names and versions; job schedulers such as pg_cron aren't on the supported list.
  • There is no OS access, true Postgres superuser or custom tablespace. databricks_superuser is a managed role, not host access. Postgres log files aren't directly accessible; logs arrive through the telemetry system tables (Beta).
  • Temporary and unlogged tables live on compute-local disk (20 GiB, or 15 GiB × the maximum CU if larger) and don't survive a restart or scale-to-zero. Cumulative statistics also reset on scale-to-zero.
  • The default collation is C.UTF-8; ICU locales are available. Encoding and collation can't change after a database is created.
Technical detail: logical replication and cutover
  • Source: wal_level = logical (on Aurora, rds.logical_replication = 1 and a reboot), a replication role and a publication, which needs superuser or the managed-service admin account. Lakebase subscribes with a role in databricks_superuser or one granted pg_create_subscription.
  • Network: the source needs a public endpoint Lakebase can reach. Private Link isn't supported, and an active account network policy must allow the source host.
  • Schema: replication copies data, not schema. Load the schema first (for example pg_dump --schema-only); later DDL isn't replicated, and foreign tables aren't copied.
  • Slot risk: the source keeps WAL until Lakebase confirms receipt, so a stalled subscription grows WAL without limit. Monitor the slot and drop the subscription after cutover.
  • Cutover: wait until every table is ready, stop writes, confirm the target is current, reset sequences with setval, then switch the app.
  • Out of Lakebase: Lakebase can publish to an external Postgres subscriber (a direct endpoint, not the pooler, allowed through the workspace IP access list). Use it for downstream systems or a rollback path. Every published table needs a primary key or replica identity.
  • Lakebase-to-Lakebase replication works only between different regions. Downtime-tolerant moves can use pg_dump and pg_restore.
  • Lakebase releases roll out in stages, a week or more after the release date for some accounts: confirm the feature in the customer's workspace before planning a cutover.
Regions in Asia Pacific

A project lives in its workspace region. AWS: ap-southeast-1 (Singapore), ap-northeast-1 (Tokyo), ap-southeast-2 (Sydney), ap-south-1 (Mumbai); not ap-east-1 (Hong Kong). Azure: East Asia (Hong Kong), Southeast Asia, Japan East, Australia East, Central India. Google Cloud (Beta): us-east4, us-central1 and europe-west3 only. Check feature and compliance availability per region; nearby regions are not interchangeable answers to a residency requirement.

Exit criteria: a rehearsed cutover, row-count and application reconciliation, a tested rollback, validated permissions and an agreed recovery plan.

Search & retrieval

Can agent memory, vector search and keyword search live in Postgres?

Yes, where the workload fits. Lakebase Search (GA) adds pgvector-compatible vector search, BM25 keyword ranking and hybrid retrieval in the same database. Since 15 Oct 2026, keyword search also segments Chinese, Japanese and Korean text.

“Keep agent state, memory and retrieval in one Postgres where that simplifies the app, then prove relevance and latency on your data.”

“You'll never need a search engine.” A second system is a workload decision, not a default.

Technical detail: search extensions and correctness
  • Enable Lakebase Search once per project (it can't be turned off), then install the extensions you need.
  • lakebase_vector adds the lakebase_ann index with pgvector-compatible types, operators and syntax, using IVF partitioning with RaBitQ quantization; Databricks documents single indexes beyond 1 billion vectors. Embedding generation stays an application or model concern.
  • lakebase_text adds BM25 ranking with top-K pushdown through lakebase_bm25 on standard tsvector data. Combine vector and keyword ranking for hybrid retrieval.
  • lakebase_tokenizer adds normalization, stopwords, synonyms and English stemming. Version 0.2.0 adds morphological analysis for Chinese, Japanese and Korean with built-in tokenizer_chinese, tokenizer_japanese and tokenizer_korean configurations; existing users run ALTER EXTENSION lakebase_tokenizer UPDATE.
  • Indexes are storage-backed, so they are ready after scale-to-zero without warm-up. BM25 corpus statistics are computed at index build and maintained by vacuum: build on populated data and validate maintenance after large loads.
  • Transactional consistency doesn't guarantee retrieval relevance. Measure recall, ranking quality, index build and maintenance cost, and p99 under concurrent writes. Apply tenant access controls to every retrieval path.

Compare recall and ranking, tenant authorization, vacuum maintenance and p99 with representative concurrent updates, in each language the app serves.

Operations

How do we monitor, patch and operate it?

Databricks runs the servers and applies patches and minor-version updates; your team owns schema, roles, sizing and app retries. Telemetry lands in Unity Catalog system tables and can be exported to your observability stack.

“Your DBAs keep the work that matters (schema, access, performance) and stop patching servers.”

“You won't need a DBA.”

Technical detail: updates, telemetry and automation
  • Updates: planned updates are posted at least 3 days ahead, and minor versions apply at the next compute restart. A restart drops sessions, so design retries.
  • Telemetry (Beta): system.lakebase tables capture query plans, session history, wait events, schema changes, database and compute health, and Postgres logs, retained for 7 days.
  • Export (Beta): OpenTelemetry metrics and logs to Grafana Cloud, New Relic, Datadog or any OTLP backend.
  • Troubleshooting (Beta): Insights surface issues and early warnings, and Genie helps investigate them. Genie Code also works inside the Lakebase project.
  • Automation: the REST API, CLI and SDKs are GA; Terraform and Databricks Asset Bundles can also configure computes.
  • Guardrails: protected branches, budget policies and tags for cost attribution, and deleted projects recoverable for 7 days. There is no host OS or superuser access.

Cost & scenarios

Is Lakebase cheaper, and what belongs in the estimate?

Sometimes; calculate the scenario. Price the production shape (compute for the primary, HA secondaries and replicas, plus storage, restore history, snapshots and sync pipelines), then compare at measured performance. Equal RAM is not equal performance.

  • Compute: CU-hours for every primary, HA secondary and read replica; the rate includes the cloud instance. A lower Always-On rate applies once scale-to-zero has been off for more than 24 consecutive hours.
  • Storage: GB-months of data, with restore history and snapshots billed separately. There is no I/O meter.
  • Sync: synced tables consume serverless pipeline DBUs.
  • Offers: the public promotion takes 50% off Lakebase compute until 31 Jan 2027 (compute only). Committed-use discounts are available.
  • Region: rates vary by cloud, region and tier; Singapore, Tokyo and Azure Hong Kong list rates are higher than US East.

A representative workload, agreed success criteria and a rehearsed failure path. A fast demo opens the door; the app's measured behavior closes it.

  2. Scaling and reconnects behave as designed.
  3. Failover and restore drills pass, with a measured recovery time.
  4. Data and security: freshness, permissions and private routes verified.
  5. Economics: the full monthly bill, including after the promotion.
  6. Delivery: a named owner, a rollout and rollback plan, and a decision date.

First app to platform

How does a first workload become the default for new apps?

Expand validated workloads, not database counts. Land one app, take it to production, repeat across teams, then make Lakebase the default for new apps.

  1. Land: one app, agent-state store, serving dataset or CI branch workflow, with agreed latency, correctness and cost targets and an owner who can take it to production.
  2. Production: validate access, recovery, observability and capacity; choose HA by the service requirement, not as a spend lever.
  3. Expand: repeat across teams or scale proven traffic. Track active production use, not the number of databases created.
  4. Standardize: shared templates, connection and retry practices, branch policies, budgets and an incident runbook. Reassess cost after the promotion ends.
  5. Migrate the estate: once new apps default to Lakebase, retire legacy databases in waves. See the big bet.

What production adoption usually looks like

  • The database decision sits with the app or platform engineering team; the data team sponsors.
  • Platform-default plays, where every new internal app gets a Postgres database, grow by count rather than through one large database.
  • Production apps run always-on, with HA where the service needs it and the minimum sized to the working set rather than padded.
  • Regulated teams check customer-managed keys and private networking early, so raise both in the first meeting.
